Goal

Turn the supplied business or product material into a specific offer and conversion brief for a public homepage or landing page. The result must tell a designer what the visitor should understand, believe and do, and why this project deserves its own visual expression. This is a planning step, not a promise of measured conversion improvement.

Inputs and evidence

Read the request, existing public/product content, relevant routes and any supplied brand or sales material. Recover the offer, audience, language, geographic scope, acquisition context, commercial terms, assets and action destinations where available. A named page or reference is evidence to inspect, not permission to copy its identity or to alter its business model.

Separate verified facts, client-provided claims, creative proposals and unknowns. Record the source for a consequential claim. Ask only for a missing fact that changes the promised outcome, eligible audience, transaction or lead destination. Ordinary layout and stylistic choices can proceed as proposals. If a decision remains unavailable, define the affected boundary and continue the independent parts of the brief.

Synthesize the offer

  1. Identify the primary visitor and their arrival context: what happened before the visit, what they are trying to accomplish, how much they already know and what they would otherwise use. For a broad homepage, name the important secondary paths without flattening all audiences into one slogan.
  2. State the offer as visitor → desired change → concrete mechanism → credible evidence → next action. Distinguish what the project actually delivers from an aspirational brand statement. Tie differentiators to a buying or adoption criterion; a technology name alone is rarely a customer benefit.
  3. Build a claim-to-proof inventory. For each important promise, identify a real demonstration, screenshot, specification, case, attributable review, comparison or constraint. Unsupported claims become questions, qualified wording or a demonstration to produce; they do not become invented statistics, partner logos or testimonials.
  4. Map the decision friction: relevance, understanding, credibility, effort, cost, risk, compatibility, timing or location. Choose which concerns this page can answer and which need a destination page, conversation or follow-up.
  5. Define the conversion contract. Name the primary action, actual destination and expected result. Match the label to its actual commitment: an enquiry requests a consultation; a booking reserves an available appointment. Preserve explicitly required wording while explaining any unresolved commitment. Define a useful secondary action for visitors who are not ready. A lead magnet needs a real deliverable, recipient expectation and delivery path; a promotion needs real conditions and expiry; a purchase CTA needs confirmed pricing and an available route.
  6. Choose an expression that belongs to the offer. Identify product behavior, craft, material, environment, cultural context or data that could produce distinctive imagery, interaction or composition. Explain the connection in a sentence so later art direction has substance beyond fashionable adjectives.

Message hierarchy

Draft a working hero proposition, a supporting explanation and an action label in the site's actual language. The opening should reveal what is offered, for whom and the useful outcome. Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over generic claims of innovation. Preserve terms users need to evaluate the offer; do not replace clarity with a clever headline that requires a second explanation.

Choose the later messages by the visitor's unanswered questions. Rank proof and differentiators by decision relevance. Keep the result compact enough to guide design; detailed copy can evolve with the section plan. Do not manufacture a sense of urgency, scarcity, certification, popularity or exclusivity.

Result and handoff

Return an offer brief containing:

  • audience and arrival context, offered change, mechanism and meaningful differentiators;
  • primary and secondary conversion paths with destinations and success states;
  • the opening message hierarchy, key objections and proof inventory;
  • brand/asset constraints, promising visual material and consequential unknowns.

Make decisions explicit enough that page architecture can select sections without inventing the offer again. Hand this brief to Web Page Architecture. A request for offer synthesis alone stops here; missing proof stays visible in the handoff.
